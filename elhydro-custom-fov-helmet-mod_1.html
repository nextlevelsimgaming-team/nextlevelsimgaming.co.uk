The problem it solves

Every headset — Crystal, Crystal Super, Dream Air, Dream Air SE — has a different sweet spot for crop and supersample. Get it wrong and you're either burning frames you didn't need to burn, or running a soft, blurry image when your GPU had headroom to spare the whole time.

As a group of VR enthusiasts, we'd been answering the same questions on repeat in the community Discord: what crop and supersample values should I actually be running, why does my headset look soft even on a high-end GPU, why am I leaving performance on the table.

Until now, the only way to know the right numbers was Elhydro's PrimaShock calculators — brilliant, but they're a spreadsheet. Pull it up, enter your headset, copy the numbers across, type them into the OpenXR toolkit by hand. It works. It's just not exactly a few clicks.

The fix: pick your headset, done

So the calculator got built straight into a custom OpenXR toolkit. Open it, pick your headset from the ELHYDRO Headset Profile list, and every value the spreadsheet used to make you work out by hand just appears — correct crop, correct resolution, supersample target calculated and applied automatically.

Performance and immersion — the same number

Here's where it comes together. The crop the toolkit applies isn't just trimming pixels off the edge of your view to save performance — it's cropping exactly to where a real helmet visor would sit. So the same math that hands your GPU its headroom back is also the shape of the helmet you're seeing. Performance and immersion aren't two separate settings here. They're the same number.

The helmet visor, explained

Run it Smooth or Scratched depending on how you want the visor to look — either way it's applying the exact FOV math Elhydro's calculators were already telling you to use. You're just seeing it now instead of reading it off a spreadsheet. On the Dream Air SE specifically, this same fix also resolves the persistence / motion-blur issue some users have seen in the current Pimax Play OpenXR runtime.

Don't want the visor on screen at all? You don't need it — Helmet mode is a toggle, not a requirement. Everything above it still applies whether you're using the visor overlay or not.

How the visor overlay actually renders: a real depth map, not a flat 2D sticker over your view. Helmet shell and chin padding sit nearest the eye, the visor surface sits further back and holds the scratch detail, and each layer is masked and offset separately so the visor reads with real depth instead of looking pasted on.

Not a 2D overlay — a simulated depth helmet, scratches and light refraction included.
