Quest 3 vs Pimax Dream Air for iRacing VR

Both headsets driven back to back at St Petersburg and Suzuka, same cars, same settings — with the field of view actually measured rather than taken off a spec sheet.

The short answer

The Dream Air is a genuine desktop-quality PC VR experience and the Quest 3 is an emulation of one. On a lossless DisplayPort connection the distant signage, braking markers and dash text that smear on the Quest 3 stay pin sharp, and at roughly 170g against 515g you can wear it for a full stint. It is also more than three times the price.

Upgrade if

  • You race seriously and read braking markers and apexes at distance
  • You still fall back to an ultrawide or triples because VR isn't sharp enough
  • You run long stints and the Quest 3's weight or battery ends your session
  • Your GPU can make use of eye-tracked foveated rendering

Don't upgrade if

  • You use the headset for standalone games and mixed reality as much as sim racing
  • You need passthrough — the Lighthouse version has none
  • You wear glasses in the headset and don't want prescription inserts
  • The price gap is money you'd rather put into a GPU or the rig itself

Clarity: compressed link versus lossless DisplayPort

The Quest 3 is a standalone headset whatever you plug into it. Running PC VR means compressing the video feed over USB or Wi-Fi, and that compression is where sim racing loses. At St Petersburg in the Ferrari 296 GT3 it holds a solid 90fps on max settings, but distant road signs and iRacing's dash text get smudged by encoding artefacts, the image has a washed-out cast, and micro stutters appear on both Wi-Fi 6E and the cable. Testing used the Virtual Desktop beta and an AMVR LC6L link cable with pass-through charging, which removes the USB-3 bandwidth and disconnect problems — so this is the Quest 3 at its best, not hobbled.

The Dream Air runs a native DisplayPort 1.4a connection, so the image arrives mathematically intact. Everything that was artefacting in the distance is sharp, and the Sony micro-OLED panels — the same ones in the Apple Vision Pro — give blacks and colour depth an LCD can't reach. No mura, no chromatic aberration, no screen door.

At Suzuka in the Porsche 911 GT3 the difference stops being cosmetic. On the Quest 3 apexes are hard to judge and braking markers often resolve only once it's too late to use them. The honest test is whether a headset can replace an ultrawide or triples: for the Quest 3 the answer is no, and for the Dream Air it's yes.

Two things worth knowing before you buy: micro-OLED panels can glare, which turning brightness down slightly reduces, and there's a brown tinge at the peripheral edge of the lenses. Both are normal across micro-OLED headsets, and both stop registering after a few sessions.

Field of view, measured

Pimax state 110° horizontal and 90° vertical. Measured with testHMD on the stock facial interface, the Dream Air gave 98° horizontal and 86° vertical. Swapping to the StudioForm X1-P brought it to 108° horizontal and 90° vertical — essentially the stated figures. The difference is lens distance, and in sim racing it's the vertical number that matters most.

Will my PC run that resolution?

The Dream Air pushes 3840×3552 per eye, which sounds impossible until you account for Tobii eye tracking, included as standard and absent entirely from the Quest 3. Dynamic foveated rendering keeps full resolution exactly where you're looking and drops the workload in your peripheral vision. In practice it's worth roughly a GPU tier — enough headroom to raise settings rather than lower them.

Outside iRacing there's more available. The PrimaShock settings put together by elhydro in the Discord have users on 4080s holding 90fps in titles like Automobilista 2.

Comfort over a full stint

The headline number is weight: roughly 170g against the Quest 3's 515g. That gap is the single biggest day-to-day difference, and there's no battery anxiety because the Dream Air runs from a mains adapter.

The stock facial interface has been uncomfortable for a lot of users, which Pimax have acknowledged — a revised interface shown at the Sim Racing Expo is shipping free to new and existing owners. In the meantime the options are the 3D-printed interface by Zeeflyboys, or the StudioForm X1-P, which comes with silk fabric cushions in varying thickness plus PU leather. The Apple Vision Pro dual strap is the other easy win and makes more difference than its price suggests.

If you wear glasses

You can't wear them in the Dream Air — the lenses sit too close. Prescription inserts are the answer, as they are on the Quest 3. The magnetic set from HONS VR, an approved Pimax partner, come with a carry case and are the ones used here.

Tracking and passthrough

You don't have to go back to lighthouses. The Dream Air supports SteamVR Lighthouse tracking if you want it, but there's also a SLAM inside-out option, so the plug-and-play convenience carries over.

Passthrough is the real culture shock coming from a Quest 3. The Lighthouse version has none at all; the SLAM version has basic black and white, fine for glancing at your surroundings and nothing more. That's normal for direct DisplayPort PC VR headsets — they aren't mixed reality devices. And no, you can't play Gorilla Tag.

The price gap

The Quest 3 has risen significantly and sits around £529 for the 512GB model in the UK. The equivalent SLAM Dream Air is around £1,774 before the ambassador discount. Whether that's worth it comes down to one question: are you after the best experience available in PC VR sim racing, or a very good headset that also does everything else? Both are defensible answers.
